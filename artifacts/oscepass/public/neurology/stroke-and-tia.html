Stroke and TIA: OSCE station

Emergency medicine8 min history2 min examiner questions20 marks
Aim and learning outcomes

Assess focused, patient-centred history taking in a time-critical presentation of suspected stroke, including collateral history from a relative.

The candidate should:

  • Open with an open question, then move to closed questions.
  • Establish the time last known well from a witness, early.
  • Cover stroke symptoms and consider mimics.
  • Screen for thrombolysis and thrombectomy safety (anticoagulants, bleeding, surgery, trauma).
  • Establish premorbid function, explore concerns, and summarise with a plan.
Candidate instructions

You are the emergency department doctor. Mr David Okafor, 66, has arrived in Resus by ambulance.

Paramedic handover: glucose 6.8 mmol/L, BP 168/94, HR 92 irregular, SpO2 97% on air, GCS 15.

Take a focused history from the patient and his wife. Do not examine. At the end, summarise and state your initial plan.

Answering patient and relative questions
QuestionModel reply
Is it a stroke?"It looks likely from what you describe. The scan will confirm it and show us the best treatment."
Will I get my arm back?"I can't promise. The sooner we treat this, the better the chance of recovery, and we are moving as fast as we can."
Is he going to die?"He is stable at the moment and we are acting quickly. I'll keep you updated at every step."
Why did this happen?"High blood pressure, diabetes and an irregular heartbeat can all cause it. Tests will tell us more."
Is the treatment risky?"A small number of people get bleeding in the brain. Without treatment the outlook is usually worse. The stroke team will go through it with you."
Why can't he eat or drink?"Swallowing can be affected, and food could go down the wrong way. We'll check it first."
Can I drive?"Not for now. The stroke team will advise when it is safe, and there are driving rules we'll go through."
Can I stay with him?"Yes, for now. A nurse will look after you and update you."

Tips: acknowledge the feeling first, use short sentences, check understanding, never over-promise, avoid false reassurance.

How to answer the examiner
  • Say your priority first: "My immediate priority is..."
  • Use a structure (ABCDE, then investigations, then treatment).
  • Give times and numbers: 4.5 hours for thrombolysis, last known well, BP under 185/110.
  • Link the answer back to this patient.
  • If unsure, say what you know and that you would ask the stroke consultant.
  • Keep to the time; stop when the examiner moves on.
Red flags for examiners
  • No last known well time.
  • Giving thrombolysis before a CT.
  • Skipping glucose or the blood thinner question.
  • False reassurance or firm promises.
  • Jargon without explanation, or interrupting the patient.
  • No summary or plan.
Examiner questions (2 minutes)
  1. What are your immediate actions and investigations?
  2. What time windows apply to reperfusion treatment?
  3. What are the contraindications and cautions for thrombolysis?
  4. What are the common stroke mimics?
  5. How would you manage a TIA with fully resolved symptoms?

Model history-taking interaction
Part 1. Introduction and consent

Doctor: Hello, I'm Dr Shah, one of the emergency doctors. Can you tell me your full name and date of birth?

Patient: David Okafor, 4th March 1960.

Doctor: Thank you, David. And you are?

Wife: Grace, his wife.

Doctor: I'd like to ask about this morning, and then we'll move quickly with tests. Is that alright with you both?

Patient: Yes, of course.

Part 2. Open question and active listening

Doctor: Could you tell me what has been happening this morning, in your own words?

Patient: I was having breakfast and my mouth felt strange. I dropped my cup, and then my left arm went heavy. My speech feels funny too.

Doctor: Thank you. Take your time. Is there anything else you've noticed?

Patient: My vision is a bit blurry on the left side. That's all.

Part 3. Collateral history and time last known well

Doctor: Grace, were you with David when this started? When was he last completely well, with no symptoms at all?

Wife: He was normal at 08:30. About ten minutes later his face drooped. I rang 999 at 08:50.

Last known well: 08:30. This is the key time for treatment decisions.

Doctor: That's very helpful. Has anything got better or worse since?

Wife: His speech is a little worse, I think.

Part 4. Signpost, then focused closed questions

Doctor: I'm going to ask some specific questions now, so short answers are fine. Any headache?

Patient: No.

Doctor: Any vomiting, double vision, dizziness or problems with balance?

Patient: No double vision. No dizziness.

Doctor: Any fit, blackout, chest pain or palpitations?

Patient: No.

Doctor: Any neck pain, or a recent neck injury or fall?

Patient: No.

Doctor: Have you ever had anything like this before, even a short episode that passed?

Patient: Never.

Part 5. Safety screen for reperfusion treatment

Doctor: Which medicines do you take?

Patient: Aspirin 75, metformin, amlodipine and a statin.

Doctor: Any other blood thinners, such as warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban or edoxaban? Any allergies?

Patient: No blood thinners, only the aspirin. No allergies.

Doctor: In the last three months, any surgery, injuries, falls, bleeding, black stools or dental extractions?

Patient: None.

Doctor: Have you ever had bleeding in the brain, or been told you have an aneurysm?

Patient: No.

Part 6. Background history

Doctor: Do you have any medical conditions? Any heart problems, such as an irregular heartbeat?

Patient: High blood pressure and diabetes. Nothing with my heart that I know of.

Wife: He fainted last year. The hospital checked him but found nothing.

Doctor: Thank you, Grace, that's useful. Does anyone in the family have strokes or heart disease? Do you smoke or drink?

Patient: My father had a stroke at 70. I stopped smoking ten years ago, and I have a few beers at weekends.

Part 7. Premorbid function and social history

Doctor: Before this morning, what could you do for yourself day to day? Who is at home with you?

Wife: Everything. He drives, does the gardening, looks after himself completely.

Patient: Just Grace and me at home.

Part 8. Ideas, concerns and expectations

Doctor: David, what do you think might be happening? What is worrying you most?

Patient: I think it might be a stroke, like my dad. Will I get my arm back? I don't want to be a burden.

Doctor: I understand. Grace, is there anything you are worried about?

Wife: Is he going to die?

Doctor: I understand why you're frightened. David is stable at the moment, and we are acting quickly, which gives him the best chance. I'll keep you both updated at every step.

Part 9. Summary and initial plan

Doctor: Let me summarise, and please correct me. David, you've had sudden left-sided weakness, slurred speech and blurry vision on the left, starting about 08:40. You were last completely well at 08:30. You take aspirin but no other blood thinners, you have high blood pressure and diabetes, and you were fully independent before today. Is that right?

Patient: Yes.

Doctor: This could be a problem with the blood supply to part of the brain, so I'm arranging an urgent CT scan of your head, a heart tracing and blood tests, and the stroke team is being alerted so they can see you straight away. Do you have any questions?

Patient: No. Thank you, doctor.

Global rating

  • Clear pass: structured, time-critical, all key points.
  • Pass: most points, minor omissions.
  • Borderline: key omission (for example no last known well time) or poor communication.
  • Fail: disorganised, unsafe omissions or unprofessional.

Common errors

  • Closed questions only.
  • Asking when symptoms were noticed, not when last well.
  • Forgetting anticoagulants and bleeding risk.
  • Not using the wife's collateral history.
  • Naming a firm diagnosis before assessment.
Examiner questions (tap each for the model answer)
1. Immediate actions and investigations?
  • ABCDE, oxygen only if hypoxic.
  • Capillary glucose to exclude hypoglycaemia.
  • FAST and NIHSS, then alert the stroke team.
  • Urgent non-contrast CT head; CT angiography if thrombectomy is considered.
  • 12-lead ECG for atrial fibrillation; bloods including clotting and group and save; IV access.
  • Monitor BP, heart rate, saturations and temperature.
2. What time windows apply to reperfusion?
  • Thrombolysis (alteplase or tenecteplase per local protocol): within 4.5 hours of onset, once haemorrhage is excluded.
  • Thrombectomy for a large vessel occlusion: generally within 6 hours; selected patients up to 24 hours from last known well, guided by advanced imaging.
  • Use the last known well time, not the time symptoms were noticed.
  • BP below 185/110 before thrombolysis and below 180/105 after.
3. Contraindications and cautions for thrombolysis?
  • Intracranial haemorrhage on CT, previous brain bleed, suspected subarachnoid haemorrhage.
  • Recent major surgery or trauma, active internal bleeding, coagulopathy.
  • Therapeutic anticoagulation (recent DOAC, raised INR on warfarin).
  • Uncontrolled hypertension, glucose outside the safe range, recent stroke or head injury.
  • Others per local protocol.
4. Common stroke mimics?
  • Hypoglycaemia (check glucose first), seizure with Todd's paresis, migraine with aura.
  • Sepsis or delirium, syncope, Bell's palsy, functional neurological disorder.
  • Old stroke deficits worsening during infection.
5. A TIA with fully resolved symptoms: management?
  • Aspirin 300 mg immediately once haemorrhage is not suspected.
  • Specialist assessment within 24 hours.
  • ECG, bloods, glucose, brain and vascular imaging as indicated.
  • Secondary prevention (statin, BP control, smoking cessation, antithrombotic therapy) and driving advice.
Teaching points
  1. Time is brain. Last known well is the most important question.
  2. Open first, then closed. The funnel captures the patient's story, then fills gaps safely.
  3. Collateral history is essential when speech or comprehension is affected.
  4. Check glucose in every suspected stroke.
  5. Never skip the safety screen for anticoagulants and bleeding risk.
  6. Premorbid function guides treatment decisions and prognosis.
  7. Be honest about uncertainty when counselling an anxious patient and relative.

Cauda equina syndrome: OSCE station

Emergency medicine8 min history2 min examiner questions20 marks
Aim and learning outcomes

Assess a sensitive, focused history in a patient with back pain and possible cauda equina syndrome, a surgical emergency where speed protects bladder, bowel and sexual function.

  • Open with an open question, then use focused closed questions.
  • Ask about saddle numbness, bladder, bowel and sexual function, sensitively and in private.
  • Cover leg symptoms and other red flags.
  • Explain the urgency in plain language and state a plan: urgent MRI and early spinal team involvement.
Candidate instructions

You are the emergency department doctor. Mr Daniel Reid, 42, has come to the department with back pain. Observations are normal. Take a focused history. Do not examine. At the end, summarise and state your initial plan.

Actor brief: patient, Mr Daniel Reid

Manner: worried, a little embarrassed about bladder and bowel questions.

After an open question: "I hurt my back lifting boxes five days ago. Now it's down both legs, and I'm struggling to pass water." After "anything else?": "My right foot keeps catching, and I feel numb in a weird place."

  • Back pain from lifting five days ago. Pain down both legs for two days, right worse. Right foot catching, no falls.
  • Since yesterday: numb around the back passage and inner thighs, can't feel toilet paper properly.
  • Bladder: hard to start, does not feel when full, dribbled this morning.
  • Bowels: constipated, did not feel the stool pass. Erections harder since yesterday (only if asked sensitively).
  • No fever, weight loss, cancer, IV drug use, recent infection or procedure, steroids or blood thinners. Only the lifting injury.
  • PMH: earlier back pain episodes, nothing serious. Takes ibuprofen and codeine. No allergies. Smokes 10 a day. Warehouse worker, lives with his partner.
  • Concerns (if asked): "Will I be paralysed? Will I work again? I can't cope with being incontinent."
Model history-taking interaction
Part 1. Introduction, consent and privacy

Doctor: Hello, I'm Dr Shah, one of the emergency doctors. Can you confirm your full name and date of birth?

Patient: Daniel Reid, 12th June 1984.

Doctor: Thank you. I'll need to ask some personal questions about your bladder and bowels. Are you comfortable here, or would you like more privacy or a chaperone?

Patient: Here is fine.

Part 2. Open question

Doctor: Could you tell me what has been happening, in your own words?

Patient: I hurt my back lifting boxes five days ago. Now it's down both legs, and I'm struggling to pass water.

Doctor: Thank you. Is there anything else you've noticed?

Patient: My right foot keeps catching, and I feel numb in a weird place.

Part 3. Saddle numbness

Doctor: I'm going to ask some specific questions now. Where exactly is the numbness? Is it around your back passage, genitals or inner thighs, the area that would touch a saddle?

Key finding: saddle numbness since yesterday.

Patient: Yes, since yesterday. When I wipe, I can't feel it properly.

Part 4. Bladder, bowel and sexual function

Doctor: Is it hard to start passing urine? Can you feel when your bladder is full? Any leaking?

Patient: Hard to start, I don't feel it's full, and I dribbled this morning.

Doctor: What about your bowels?

Patient: Constipated, and I didn't feel it when I went.

Doctor: This next question is sensitive, but important. Any change in erections or sexual function?

Patient: Yes, harder since yesterday.

Part 5. Legs and other red flags

Doctor: Is the leg pain in one leg or both? Any weakness, numbness or falls?

Patient: Both, the right is worse. My foot catches. No falls.

Doctor: Any fever, weight loss, history of cancer, injecting drugs, recent infection or procedure, steroids or blood thinners?

Patient: None of those.

Part 6. Background and social

Doctor: Have you had back problems before? What medicines do you take? Any allergies? What do you do for work, and who is at home?

Patient: Some back aches before, nothing serious. Ibuprofen and codeine, no allergies. I work in a warehouse and live with my partner. I smoke ten a day.

Part 7. Ideas, concerns and expectations

Doctor: What is worrying you most?

Patient: Will I be paralysed? Will I work again? I can't cope with being incontinent.

Doctor: Those are understandable worries. This is why we are acting quickly, because treating pressure on the nerves early gives the best chance of recovery.

Part 8. Summary and plan

Doctor: Let me summarise. Daniel, you hurt your back five days ago, and now have pain down both legs, a right foot that catches, numbness around your back passage, trouble passing urine, reduced bowel sensation and a change in sexual function. I'm worried this could be pressure on the bundle of nerves at the bottom of the spine, called cauda equina syndrome. It is an emergency. I'm arranging a bladder scan and an urgent MRI of your spine, and calling the spinal team now. I'll also examine you, including checking feeling and muscle tone around the back passage, with a chaperone. Is that alright?

Patient: Yes. Thank you.

Teaching points
  1. Ask about saddle numbness and bladder and bowel function in every back pain history.
  2. Create privacy and ask sensitively.
  3. Early symptoms can be subtle: altered sensation, hesitancy, not just retention.
  4. It is an emergency: early MRI and spinal team.
  5. Never reassure falsely or discharge on painkillers when red flags are present.
  6. Document a clear neurological baseline.
